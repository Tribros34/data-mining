Math Buddy is a tablet tutor for elementary school math. It reads what the child writes, hears what the child says, watches whether the child is paying attention, and keeps track of how much of every topic the child has learned.

Every function of the tutor is one machine learning block. Six blocks are supervised (they learned from labelled examples); B7 and B8 are unsupervised (they find groups without labels). This page lets you feed each block your own input and see its output, independently of the others.

Things to notice

  • soccer was deliberately left out of the training data. TF-IDF only knows the words it has seen, so an unknown word carries no information; DistilBERT would know that soccer ≈ football.
  • Interests: sports, animals, food, games. Training data: ~1,600 synthetic messages with typos and 8% noisy sentiment labels.

B3

Handwritten digit recognition

VisualSupervised classificationMNIST · Random Forest

The child writes the answer with a finger. Draw a digit in the square; it is prepared like an MNIST image (crop, thicken, fit into 20×20, centre in 28×28) and read by the MNIST model. The small 8×8 model from the first part of the notebook is shown next to it for comparison.

Input · drawing

Draw one digit, 0–9.

Things to notice

  • The 8×8 model scores 97% on its own test set but misreads most real drawings: 8×8 pixels are too few (the hole of a 6 disappears) and drawings look different from the scanned training digits. This is notebook section B3.8.
  • Digits written very thin, very small or touching the edge are harder. MNIST digits are thick strokes, centred.

Things to notice

  • How the camera measures (MediaPipe Face Landmarker, 478 face points + eye scores): the first 2 seconds record your “looking at the screen” direction. Head angle = average angle between your face direction and that direction over the last 10 s. Looking at the screen = share of frames in the last 10 s with a face, turned less than 20°, eyes not looking sideways. Blink rate = blinks per minute over the last 30 s. If the face is missing for most of the window, the value is sent as missing.
  • Expect surprises. B4 was trained on synthetic numbers that we assumed, not on real camera measurements. A real person's normal blink rate or head movement may sit near another class – the gap between assumed and measured data is a limitation for the report.
  • Missing values are filled with the training median (SimpleImputer) before the forest sees them, so the prediction with a missing value leans towards the typical case.
  • Data is synthetic, with overlapping classes on purpose. Real alternative: DAiSEE / EngageNet engagement datasets.
B5a

Spoken digit recognition

AudioSupervised classificationMFCC + Logistic Regression

The child says the answer aloud. The recording becomes 40 numbers (mean and spread of 20 MFCC coefficients) and a Logistic Regression picks the digit. Say one English digit (“zero” … “nine”), or play a sample from the dataset.

Input · microphone
The browser will ask for the microphone.
Or · upload a .wav Or · a dataset sample (speaker “george”)

Record or choose a sample.

Things to notice

  • Expect mistakes on your own voice. The model knows only six adult speakers. On a speaker it has never heard, accuracy drops from 92% to about 50% (notebook B5a.8). Ten calibration recordings per digit from the new speaker raise it to ~82% (B5a.9).
  • Your recording is resampled to 8 kHz and the silence around the word is cut, because the dataset recordings are cut tightly. The samples were possibly part of the training data, so they are easy.

Things to notice

  • How the microphone measures (librosa, on the server): loudness is computed every 10 ms; louder than the background = speech. Time before answering = from the moment the question appears to the first speech. Pauses = silences longer than 0.25 s between the first and the last speech. Speech rate = loudness peaks (≈ syllables) per second of the answer. Pitch variation = spread of the voice pitch (pYIN) in semitones. B5a also tries to read the last word as a digit.
  • Expect surprises. The training data is synthetic: we assumed these numbers, nobody measured them on children. Counting syllables from loudness peaks is approximate, and an adult voice or a noisy room shifts the values.
  • The strongest signal is the delay before answering, then the speech rate and the pauses (coefficients in the notebook, B5b.5).
  • Synthetic data. Real alternative: the Harvard Uncertainty speech corpus.

The problem

A brand-new student has no answers yet, so B6 can only say “like the average student”. This is the cold start problem. Every topic needs its own evidence: five correct additions say nothing about fractions.

The solution in Math Buddy

Day 0 is a placement test: 5 medium questions per topic, 25 in total, before the normal lessons begin. The curve on the right (notebook section B6.7, 40 unseen test students) shows why 5: after about five answers of a topic the error stops dropping quickly.

Things to notice

  • A new student starts at the average of all students. Each topic needs about five answers before the estimate becomes personal (cold start, notebook B6.7). The day-0 placement test exists for this.
  • Answers given while distracted count less: a wrong answer while distracted is partly explained by the distraction.
  • Real alternative dataset: ASSISTments.

Things to notice

  • The silhouette score is only about 0.2: children do not fall into a few separate types, their traits vary continuously. K-Means still gives a useful segmentation into four groups a teacher can act on.
  • Learning speed is the slope of a per-student learning curve (topic, difficulty and attention held fixed), not the mastery gain, which made weak starters look like fast learners.
  • Real alternative dataset: OULAD (Open University Learning Analytics Dataset).
